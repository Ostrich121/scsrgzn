IIC (2025) 56:1734–1751 https://doi.org/10.1007/s40319-025-01624-2 ARTICLE Can Copyright Law Beneﬁt from the Marking Requirement of the AI Act? Kateryna Militsyna

Accepted: 18 June 2025 / Published online:  The Author(s) 2025

Abstract Advancements in generative artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) have raised signiﬁcant challenges for copyright law. These include the issue of distinguishing between copyrightable and non-copyrightable AI-based output and the risk of copyfraud. One potential solution that might be envisaged in this regard is to subject non-copyrightable AI-based output to a transparency obligation. While these questions remain a topic of debate within copyright law, an answer may have already crystallised beyond its boundaries. In this vein, the article attempts to elucidate whether copyright law, in its quest to address the complexities at hand, can beneﬁt from the marking requirement under Art. 50(2) AI Act. To this end, the article provides an overview of this provision, explores the technical and legal challenges associated with it, and analyses its prospects for copyright law. The article concludes that, despite its appeal, Art. 50(2) AI Act is unlikely to constitute a solution for differentiating between copyrightable and non-copyrightable AI-based output and combating copyfraud, and points to other approaches that are being discussed in this context.

1 Introduction With the growing prominence of generative artiﬁcial intelligence1 (AI) in creative domains, the range of content that is produced using AI is expanding rapidly. In addition, while modern generative AI models and systems are becoming increasingly sophisticated, they are also becoming more accessible and easier to use. These developments have lowered the barrier to content creation and enabled individuals without specialised expertise to engage in this activity.2 However, such advancements have also given rise to a number of challenges. With regard to copyright law, they have prompted questions about the sufﬁciency of human creative contribution to AI-based output.3 While differing opinions may exist, it is the position of the author of this article that the use of generative AI to produce outcomes does not automatically render them non-copyrightable. The use of generative AI encompasses varying levels of involvement and, in some instances, the degree of human creative contribution to creating output may prove sufﬁcient to satisfy the criteria for copyright protection. It follows that there can be copyrightable and noncopyrightable AI-based output.4 This brings us to another challenge that generative AI poses to copyright law. As generative AI models and systems continue to advance, non-copyrightable AI-based outcomes become increasingly work-like, making it difﬁcult to distinguish them from‘‘works’’5 in the absence of information about their origin. In light of the foregoing, when AI-based output is not copyrightable but work-like, it becomes all the more tempting to conceal the AI origin6 and exploit the resemblance to works in order to secure copyright protection, which would otherwise be unavailable.7 One potential solution to address the outlined risk of copyfraud8 may consist in subjecting non-copyrightable AI-based output to a transparency obligation.9 The realisation of this idea would require the prior establishment of a boundary between copyrightable and non-copyrightable AI-based output. Without this distinction, legal uncertainty would arise regarding which outcomes fall under the transparency obligation. Notably, the recently adopted Artiﬁcial Intelligence Act10 (AI Act), which attributes a signiﬁcant role to transparency, provides for a two-tiered11 obligation pertaining to the output.12 The ﬁrst tier relates to the marking of AI-generated and AI-manipulated13 content (Art. 50(2)),14 whereas the second tier entails an additional disclosure obligation that only concerns deep fakes and AI-generated and AI-manipulated texts‘‘published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest’’(Art. 50(4)).15 This article focuses on the ﬁrst tier and endeavours to elucidate whether copyright law, in its pursuit of differentiating between copyrightable and non-copyrightable AI-based output and combating copyfraud, can beneﬁt from the corresponding obligation. To this end, the article unfolds as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of the marking requirement under Art. 50(2) AI Act. Section 3 considers the technical challenges associated with this provision, while Sect. 4 explores the legal challenges accompanying it. Section 5 goes on to analyse whether copyright law can leverage Art. 50(2) AI Act to draw a distinction between copyrightable and non-copyrightable AI-based output and to combat copyfraud.

Section 6 then points to other approaches that are being discussed in this regard. Section 7 concludes that, despite its appeal, Art. 50(2) AI Act is unlikely to serve as a solution to the copyright-related challenges at hand.

As Recital 133 AI Act suggests, the marking requirement was designed in response to‘‘new risks of misinformation and manipulation at scale, fraud, impersonation and consumer deception’’73 (veracity risks).74 Moreover, it is expected to facilitate the effective implementation of the Digital Services Act75 and, ultimately, the prevention of‘‘the algorithmic ampliﬁcation of harmful content’’.76 Admittedly, addressing these risks is of paramount importance, particularly in light of the anticipation that misinformation and disinformation will become the most signiﬁcant threats by 2026.77 Although copyright-related risks are not mentioned in connection with the said requirement, it is interesting to explore whether Art. 50(2) AI Act still offers some opportunities to address them. Such opportunities may potentially exist in empowering copyright enforcement,78 delineating copyrightable/ non-copyrightable AI-based output and combating copyfraud. This article focuses on the latter two aspects.

What motivates the author of this article to explore whether Art. 50(2) AI Act can be useful in addressing delineation and copyfraud, despite these issues not being mentioned in the AI Act? The answer to this question lies with the approach taken by the EU legislator with regard to the marking requirement in question. In considering the framing of a transparency obligation pertaining to output, one encounters two distinct yet not mutually exclusive approaches: process-based and impact-based. As the name suggests, a process-based approach is centered on the provenance of content, that is to say, on the process through which the outcome was produced or modiﬁed. In contrast, an impact-based approach is concerned with the potential consequences that may be caused by the content.79 Given the risks identiﬁed in the AI Act, one might expect the EU legislator to prioritise veracity and opt for an impact-based approach. However, the transparency obligation set forth in Art. 50(2) AI Act is centred on the process of the content creation. It stipulates that the marking must convey whether the output is artiﬁcially generated or manipulated. Although the information about the use of AI in the production or alteration of the output may still give rise to doubts about the content’s veracity,80 the focus of the pertinent provision remains on the process.

The application of the marking requirement depends on whether AI systems are used beyond performing‘‘an assistive function for standard editing’’or making insubstantial alterations to‘‘the input data provided by the deployer or the semantics thereof’’.82 One may draw a parallel between these two approaches. Speciﬁcally, the exceptions for standard editing and insubstantial alteration may be seen as corresponding to the retention by a human of sufﬁcient creative contribution to AI-based output. One might go beyond merely drawing a parallel and consider whether determining if synthetic content falls under one of the exceptions or is subject to the marking requirement could help clarify the output’s eligibility for copyright protection.

A subsequent idea that Art. 50(2) AI Act can beneﬁt copyright law in addressing copyfraud may be situated against the backdrop that: (i) it is through the inquiry into the creation process that works and AI-based work-like output can be differentiated;

(ii) it is the content’s creator who has the knowledge about the creation process and use of generative AI, and thus may be in a position to withhold relevant information;

(iii) the marking requirement may render the act of copyfraud, if not impossible, then at least more difﬁcult to commit provided that the marking is not easily removable or susceptible to tampering.

In such a context, it may be tempting to hypothesise that Art. 50(2) AI Act, beyond the veracity risks, may assist in responding to the outlined challenges posed by AI-based output to copyright law.83 Yet, upon further reﬂection, it appears that this provision is unlikely to be the appropriate means of resolving the aforementioned copyright Gordian knots for the following reasons.

The potential of Art. 50(2) AI Act to hold some promise for the stated copyright issues hinges largely on the interpretation of standard editing and insubstantial alteration exceptions. As previously noted, the contours of these exceptions are presently ambiguous but are anticipated to become more clearly deﬁned over time. Until that occurs, one is left with own speculations. To elucidate this idea, it might be helpful to consider some examples. Let us contemplate a scenario in which an image requires editing. If an AI-powered image editing tool is used solely to make the colours of a photo a little warmer, this poses no signiﬁcant risk neither to copyrightability nor to veracity and, therefore, does not require the marking of the content. One may also add smiles to the faces of individuals depicted in the photo and change the colour of their eyes. However, are they still considered‘‘standard editing’’under Art. 50(2) AI Act, or do they trigger the marking requirement? Even if the future interpretation of the standard editing exception is not overly restrictive and includes the actions mentioned, the bigger challenges may lie with the insubstantial alteration exception. What will be the outcome of the assessment when an AI-powered translator is used to accurately translate the text without distorting its meaning, and the translation requires little to no modiﬁcation? From a copyright law perspective, the human creative contribution is insufﬁcient, and the translation is not copyrightable if a person merely‘‘ratiﬁes’’the ﬁnal result.84 In this case, it may be in the interests of copyright law to be‘‘informed’’of the AI origin of the translation to prevent it from being falsely claimed as human-authored and protected by copyright. The assessment of the translation pursuant to Art. 50(2) AI Act may offer an alternative perspective. Assuming the translation is accurate and the information conveyed by the text remains unchanged, there are no associated veracity risks. Does it imply that the insubstantial alteration exception is applicable, while the marking requirement is not? If the pertinent assessment has indeed room for consideration of veracity risks, then the answer will be afﬁrmative. Additionally, as previously noted, the insubstantial alteration exception relates to the input data provided by the deployer, the so-called‘‘professional user’’. In contrast, when it comes to determining the eligibility of an outcome for copyright protection, copyright law is indifferent to the professional status of the person. Its primary concern is that the author is a natural person, though some national copyright laws permit exceptions to this general rule.85 Even these few examples illustrate that the results of the copyrightability assessment and the evaluation under Art. 50(2) AI Act may diverge. 6 If Not Art. 50(2) AI Act, Then What?

If Art. 50(2) AI Act cannot be used to address copyfraud, what recourse remains available? One may think of introducing a copyright-speciﬁc marking (machinereadable) or disclosure (human-facing) requirement. While outwardly appealing, this idea is hardly viable at present. This is due to its dependence on widespread adoption,87 susceptibility to circumvention,88 and difﬁculties in oversight and enforcement.89 On top of that, any potential marking requirement would encounter the technical issues previously outlined. Distinguishing between copyrightable and non-copyrightable AI-based output is another major challenge. Against the backdrop that the use of AI to generate output does not automatically result in non-copyrightability, imposing a duty to disclose any use of AI does not appear justiﬁed in the context of copyright. For this reason, introducing a copyright-speciﬁc marking or disclosure requirement would be more relevant for non-copyrightable AI-based output. That said, this approach would necessitate ﬁrst resolving the long-debated issue of delineation.90 Another potential solution to copyfraud, which has not

Although the said requirement is process-based, this article puts forth the proposition that its rationale is impact-based and lies in addressing the veracity risks. Conversely, copyright law does not concern itself with veracity. In fact, it centers on human creativity, with ﬁctional settings common to its subject matter. These differences may result in divergent assessments and determinations regarding the necessity of marking content. While copyright law is concerned with the AI origin of output when human creative contribution is deemed insufﬁcient, the marking requirement under Art. 50(2) AI Act may not be triggered in the same case if there are no veracity risks. Likewise, veracity distortion could lead to the need for marking as prescribed in Art. 50(2) AI Act even when copyright law does not regard the use of AI in content generation or manipulation as relevant.
